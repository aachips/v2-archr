## One Person's Story

Several years ago, I knew someone who worked for a small farming non-profit in a rural town. Each week, they would drive to the local farmer's market and set up a Donation Station. At the end of the day, after the merchants had packed up, they would collect thousands of pounds of leftover produce—the imperfect vegetables, the bruised fruit, the surplus that no one wanted to haul back. They would then drive that food to the people and places that needed it most: shelters, food banks, community kitchens.

When I asked them about their work, they said something I've never forgotten.

> _"I wish my job was obsolete."_

They didn't mean they wanted to be unemployed. They meant they wished their job _didn't need to exist_. They wished our society was organized well enough that fresh, nutritious food found its way to people without needing a dedicated Donation Station person. They wished the farmers could sell everything. They wished the shelters had steady supply without reliance on bruised tomatoes and day-old bread.

Their job was a beautiful, necessary thing—and a _failure of our systems_. They lived in that contradiction every single day.

## The Same Story, In a Different Field

I told this story to my boss. He leads our coalition of home repair organizations. Before he was in an office, he was on a roof. He did the real work, the hands-on work, the work that you can see and feel and point to at the end of the day.

Now he is a manager. He sits in meetings. He moves numbers between spreadsheets. He reconciles funding sources and drafts contracts and tracks metrics and attends coordination calls.

He told me something that echoed the Donation Station conversation.

> _"The fact that my job exists is a joke. It's an embarrassment. An indictment on how bad we are as a society."_

He didn't mean his job wasn't important. He meant it _shouldn't be necessary_. He meant the work of coordinating a disaster response—the spreadsheets, the eligibility tracking, the funding reconciliation, the endless emails—should be handled by machines. By robots. By systems that don't need lunch breaks, don't get tired, and don't have to choose between taking a vacation and letting the operation collapse.

He said his job is like a "hellish purgatory." He used to help people directly. Now he helps people by making sure other people can help people—and the distance from the roof to the office has been hard on his soul.

> _"If I leave, the operation would fall apart. But I don't want to do this forever. I'd rather be almost anywhere else."_

He is the lynchpin of a group doing extraordinary work in a time of extraordinary need. And he would gladly trade his role for a world where it wasn't needed.

## The Hard Lines

But—and this is critical—he drew hard lines.

> _"Robots shouldn't be making determinations on people's home repair fate. Robots shouldn't be talking to clients over the phone or email."_

That's where the automation stops. That's the boundary that cannot be crossed.

The system can sort, categorize, organize, track, and remind. It can surface flags, highlight inconsistencies, and suggest next steps. It can take the mind-numbing, soul-crushing spreadsheet work off a human's plate.

But the _judgment_—the decision about whether someone gets help, the conversation with a traumatized family, the handshake, the eye contact, the "we're here for you"—that belongs to people.

The system says: _"Here are the green flags. Here are the red flags. Here's what we know, and here's what we don't know."_

The human says: _"Yes, we can help you."_

## Our Approach: "Assume Eligibility"

This philosophy is baked into our eligibility engine.

We don't have the system reject applications. We have the system reveal—so that humans can decide.

When an application comes in, the system runs its checks:

- Income: ✓ (within range)
    
- Geography: ⚠️ (ZIP code is eligible but county has limited capacity)
    
- Demographics: ✓ (veteran household)
    
- Damage: ✓ (Helene-related)
    
- Previous assistance: ❓ (address appears in historical records, need human verification)
    

The system doesn't say: "DENIED."

The system says: "Here's what we found. Here are the flags that need a second look. And here are the organizations that are a good fit, based on what we know so far."

The final determination—the "yes" that changes a family's life—comes from a person. A caseworker. A project manager. Someone who can look at the whole picture, not just the checkboxes.

## What This Means for the Software We're Building

This platform has a purpose beyond efficiency.

**We are automating the tedious so that humans can focus on the human.**

The system handles:

- Submissions and notifications
    
- Eligibility checks (with flags, not final decisions)
    
- Document generation and storage
    
- Task tracking and reminders
    
- Funding reconciliation
    
- Communication logs
    

The system does not handle:

- Final eligibility determinations
    
- Client conversations
    
- Emotional support
    
- The "yes" or the "no"
    
- Community connection

## A Better World

When we build this system, we are building toward a world where:

- The person on the roof can focus on the repair
    
- The caseworker can focus on the family
    
- The project manager can focus on the project
    
- The volunteer can focus on the person
    

And the manager, the coordinator, the person drowning in spreadsheets—they can either do more human work, or they can move on to something else. Something that brings them closer to the work that matters.

> _"I wish my job was obsolete."_

We are building the machine that makes that possible. Not because we want anyone to be without work. Because we want every person to do work that matters.

---

## The Tradeoff

Some people will say: "If you automate this, you're taking away someone's livelihood."

But if the job is spreadsheet-wrangling, and the person wants to be on a roof—or at a farmer's market, or in a classroom, or anywhere else—then automating the spreadsheet work is an act of liberation, not theft.

**The real theft is keeping capable, passionate people trapped in work that doesn't need a human.**

We owe it to each other to build systems that handle the mechanical so people can handle the meaningful.

## One Last Thought

The person at the farmer's market eventually left the non-profit. They moved on to work at a community health clinic. They still do beautiful, necessary work. And they still wish, every day, that their job didn't need to exist.

But they're closer to the people they serve. They're not hauling produce anymore. They're building relationships.

That's the direction we should all be heading.
