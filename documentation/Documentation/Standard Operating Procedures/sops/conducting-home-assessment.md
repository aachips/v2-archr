# Conducting a Home Assessment

> **Who this is for:** Assessors who visit properties to evaluate repair needs and create estimates. Also useful for Caseworkers who coordinate assessments.

## Why This Matters

Quality assessments determine accurate estimates and successful repairs. A good assessment means the right crew shows up with the right materials. A bad assessment means they show up, realize the problem is twice as big as expected, and leave — and the applicant waits longer.

**Your assessment is the blueprint for everything that follows.** Get it right and the whole project flows. Get it wrong and everyone pays for it — in time, money, and frustration.

---

## Before You Go: Preparation Checklist

- [ ] **Review the application** — what repair needs did the applicant report?
- [ ] **Check eligibility flags** — any red or yellow flags you should know about?
- [ ] **Confirm the address** — placecode and property address should match
- [ ] **Contact the applicant** — schedule a time that works for them
- [ ] **Charge your phone/tablet** — you'll be taking photos and filling out the assessment form
- [ ] **Bring measuring tools** — tape measure, level, moisture meter if available
- [ ] **Bring safety gear** — hard hat, flashlight, gloves if entering damaged areas

> **Pro tip:** Ask the applicant to gather any insurance documents, FEMA correspondence, or contractor estimates they may have. This saves a follow-up trip.

## On-Site: Step-by-Step Assessment

### 1. Start with the Exterior

Walk the property perimeter first. Look for:
- Roof damage (missing shingles, sagging, visible holes)
- Siding damage (missing panels, water stains, warping)
- Foundation issues (cracks, settling, water intrusion)
- Gutter and drainage problems
- Tree damage affecting the structure

### 2. Assess Each Room Systematically

For each room, check:
- **Walls** — water damage, mold, structural cracks
- **Floors** — warping, soft spots, water damage
- **Ceiling** — stains, sagging, active leaks
- **Windows/doors** — operable, sealed, damaged
- **Electrical** — outlets working, visible wiring damage
- **Plumbing** — water pressure, visible leaks, water heater condition

### 3. Document with Photos

**The photo rule:** Every repair need gets at least two photos — one wide shot showing context, one close-up showing the specific issue.

**Photo best practices:**
- Turn on location services — photos with GPS data are far more useful
- Include a reference object (ruler, hand, coin) for scale
- Take "before" photos from the same angle you'll use for "after"
- Label photos in the system with room + issue (e.g., "Kitchen - ceiling water damage")

> **Important:** Photos aren't just documentation. They're evidence for grant reporting, insurance claims, and proving the work was done. A case without photos is a case that can't be closed properly.

### 4. Fill Out the Assessment Form

In the platform (mobile app or desktop):

1. **Open the assigned case**
2. **Navigate to the Assessment section**
3. **Select each repair category** that applies (roof, plumbing, electrical, etc.)
4. **Describe the damage** — be specific. "Roof leaking" is not as useful as "3' × 4' section of missing shingles on east-facing slope, active water intrusion into attic"
5. **Estimate severity** — minor, moderate, severe, or urgent
6. **Note any safety hazards** — structural instability, exposed wiring, mold

### 5. Create a Preliminary Estimate

For each repair need, note:
- **What trade(s) are required** — roofing, plumbing, electrical, carpentry, etc.
- **Rough scope** — how much work? (e.g., "Replace 40 sq ft of subfloor in bathroom")
- **Materials needed** — approximate types and quantities
- **Estimated labor hours** — your best guess based on experience

> **You don't need exact numbers here.** A rough estimate is fine — the contractor or crew lead will refine it. But "I don't know" doesn't help anyone. Use your professional judgment.

## What If the Applicant Disagrees?

Sometimes an applicant thinks their repair need is bigger (or smaller) than your assessment says. This is normal.

**How to handle it:**
1. **Listen** — the applicant knows their home better than you do
2. **Explain your findings** — walk them through what you observed
3. **Document the disagreement** — note it in the assessment. "Applicant reports additional leaking in basement not visible during assessment — recommend follow-up inspection"
4. **Don't argue** — if there's uncertainty, flag it for the caseworker or PM to investigate

**Remember:** The applicant has been through a disaster. They're stressed. Patience goes a long way.

---

## After the Assessment

1. **Submit the assessment** in the platform
2. **Log a progress event** — "Assessment completed by [Your Name]"
3. **Notify the caseworker** — they should follow up with the applicant to discuss findings
4. **Upload any additional photos** you didn't capture in the assessment form

## Quick Reference: Assessment Categories

| Category | What to Look For | Typical Trades Needed |
|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Roof** | Missing shingles, leaks, structural damage | Roofer |
| **Plumbing** | Leaks, no water, damaged pipes | Plumber |
| **Electrical** | Exposed wiring, non-functional outlets, panel damage | Electrician |
| **HVAC** | No heat/AC, damaged ductwork | HVAC technician |
| **Structural** | Foundation cracks, wall bowing, floor sagging | General contractor |
| **Interior** | Water damage, mold, damaged drywall | Carpenter, painter |
| **Accessibility** | Ramps, door widths, bathroom modifications | General contractor |
| **Safety** | Exposed hazards, entry security | Various |
